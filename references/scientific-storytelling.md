# Scientific storytelling and explanation design

## Design the argument

Make the deck understandable from its action titles alone. Each title should express the slide's conclusion, relationship, or question. Avoid topic labels such as "Method", "Results", or "Background" unless paired with the actual point.

Maintain one primary claim per slide. A second or third claim may support it, but split the slide when independent ideas compete for attention.

## Match visuals to reasoning

| Reasoning task | Preferred visual |
|---|---|
| Explain a mechanism | staged process, state transition, or causal chain |
| Derive an equation | term-by-term construction with persistent color identity |
| Compare methods | aligned axes, synchronized states, or small multiples |
| Present evidence | chart with units, baseline, uncertainty, and direct annotation |
| Explain an algorithm | data state -> operation -> updated state on one example |
| Establish scale | familiar reference, unit ladder, or progressive magnitude |
| State limitations | boundary diagram, failure region, or assumption checklist |

Use animation to reveal dependency, transformation, or attention order. Do not animate merely to keep the screen moving.

## Equations

- Define symbols before transforming the equation.
- Preserve the color of a semantic quantity across equations and diagrams.
- Change one conceptual operation at a time.
- Keep the prior state visible when the audience must compare steps.
- Pair formal notation with an intuitive representation when possible.
- Pause on the final form and state what it enables.

## Experimental results

- State the comparison and metric before showing values.
- Keep axes, units, sample sizes, and uncertainty visible.
- Highlight the result being discussed and mute unrelated series.
- Avoid truncated axes unless explicitly justified.
- Separate reported measurements from projections or simulations.
- Connect the result back to the research question, not merely to an improvement percentage.

## Pacing

- Open with the decision, contradiction, or phenomenon—not an agenda slide.
- Spend more time on the novel mechanism than on generic background.
- Insert short recaps after difficult sections.
- Use transitions to re-establish the question and accumulated evidence.
- End with contribution, evidence, limitation, and implication; keep it visible for questions.

For research-results talks, follow the contribution and its supporting evidence, adjusting background to the audience. A worked example is useful when it clarifies the method; exercises and prerequisite-by-prerequisite progression are appropriate when the user requests a lesson.

## Citations

Place compact source IDs or author-year citations on the slide whenever a figure, quantitative claim, or borrowed formulation appears. Put full references in notes or final reference slides. Ensure citations remain readable in PDF poster frames.
